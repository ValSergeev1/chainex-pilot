The dispatch board

The desk that stays with the route.

The dispatch board is the place a coordinator works the day: what needs a person, what is ready to move, and what is already on the road. This page describes that work from the desk’s point of view, and how it supports drivers, shippers, and the partners who share the same day.

A good dispatch board does not ask a coordinator to hunt. It puts the work that needs a person at the front, keeps the rest of the day in view, and stays in step with what drivers are actually doing on the road.

Seeing the day clearly

The board opens on the state of the operation, in language a person can use: how many routes are running, and how many items need someone at the desk. The work itself is arranged so a coordinator can tell, quickly, what is waiting for a decision, what is ready to be assigned, what is already moving, and what is finished. That picture is the same one the rest of the team is working from, so a conversation with a driver or a partner starts from shared facts rather than from a private list.

Problems are listed first. A missed window, a driver who needs help, a stop that cannot be completed, a person who is not yet cleared to take a route — those belong at the top of the day, not at the bottom of a long table. The board is designed so the next useful action is close at hand, rather than hidden behind a row of tools.

Assigning work, and staying with it

Once work is ready, a coordinator can assign it and keep an eye on it as it moves. The map is available without taking over the screen, so the desk can understand where a route is without losing the list they were just using. When a driver updates a stop, reports a problem, or changes the order of the work, the board reflects it. The point is not to watch every turn. The point is that when the desk is needed, it is needed with the current state of the route, not a picture from an hour ago.

The same board works on a phone. A coordinator who has to step away from the desk can still see what needs attention, take a simple action, and come back without having to reconstruct the day. The layout changes to fit a smaller screen; the idea does not.

Working with drivers in the moment

Drivers and the desk are in the same loop. If a driver asks for help because a route has too many stops, or reports that a delivery could not be completed, that request lands where a coordinator is already looking. Approving a change, answering a question, or sending a route onward should not require opening a second system or waiting until a later report arrives.

This is also how the board protects the day. Some people should not be given a route until they are cleared to drive. The desk should see that before work is assigned, not after a driver is already on the road. Those checks belong in the same place as the rest of the day’s decisions, so they are part of the work instead of a separate reminder.

Who this helps

Drivers

A driver who needs the desk should not have to explain the whole route from scratch. When the board already shows the stop, the issue, and the current plan, the help they get can be specific. Changes they make to their own map are visible, so they are not talking at cross-purposes with the person trying to help them.

Shippers

Shippers feel dispatch when a window is at risk, a delivery cannot be completed, or a customer is waiting on an answer. A board that keeps exceptions in front of a coordinator, and that stays in step with the driver, shortens the time between something going wrong and someone being able to speak about it clearly.

DSPs and carriers

Partners who provide the people and the capacity need a desk that can see across the work, not only inside one route. When assignments, problems, and progress live on one board, a DSP or carrier can understand how their drivers are being used, where help is needed, and whether the day is closing as expected — without waiting for an end-of-day summary to catch up with the street.
